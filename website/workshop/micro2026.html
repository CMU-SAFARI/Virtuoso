01 Why Virtuoso

About the workshop and tutorial

Building on the success of our workshops at MICRO 2025 and ASPLOS 2026, we are excited to bring the Virtuoso Workshop to MICRO 2026 in Athens!

This third edition continues to explore the frontier of hardware/OS co-design for memory management, featuring new talks, updated demos, and fresh perspectives from leading researchers.

Traditional computing systems face significant challenges due to rigid interfaces between hardware and operating systems (OS). These interfaces struggle to meet the performance, efficiency, and security demands of modern applications. For example, the growth in data requirements has turned virtual memory (VM) into a major performance bottleneck.

This has led to a paradigm shift towards hardware/OS co-design, where hardware components and OS mechanisms are designed in tandem to optimize the system. This tutorial and workshop will provide a comprehensive introduction to this area, focusing on memory management.

A core component will be a hands-on exploration of Virtuoso, a simulation framework that enables rapid prototyping and evaluation of HW/OS co-designs. Published at ASPLOS 2025, Virtuoso provides a practical environment for attendees to experiment with co-design strategies and gain practical skills. The workshop is designed for students, engineers, and researchers in computer architecture and operating systems.

03 Invited speakers

Chloe Alverti

NTUA

The Renaissance of Distributed Shared Memory Systems

Abstract

Distributed Shared Memory (DSM), a long-standing vision in computer systems, is being revisited as emerging interconnects such as CXL enable low-latency, cache-coherent memory access across nodes within a rack. This technological shift invites us to rethink fundamental systems interfaces: how processes and their state move across machines, how shared-memory applications scale in distributed environments, and how DSM systems remain resilient in the presence of failures. Realizing this vision requires advances in both software and hardware, often through their co-design. This talk highlights three recent developments along this path. At the software layer, CXLfork introduces a zero-serialization, near-zero-copy remote fork mechanism by checkpointing process state directly into shared CXL memory. At the hardware layer, PhasedStore and Diktamo address a fundamental challenge in cache-coherent DSM systems: resilience to node failures. In such systems, node crashes can result in the silent loss of dirty shared data buffered in private CPU caches. PhasedStore mitigates this risk by enabling high-performance write-through coherence protocols that propagate dirty data into a fault-tolerant domain. Diktamo takes a complementary approach by replicating dirty cache lines across the rack to ensure their availability in the event of node failures. Together, these developments outline a path toward practical, high-performance, and fault-tolerant distributed shared memory systems.

Bio

Chloe Alverti is a postdoctoral researcher at the Computing Systems Laboratory (CSLab) of the National Technical University of Athens (NTUA). Her research interests span operating systems, computer architecture, and hardware–software co-design, with a particular focus on memory systems for over a decade. She received her Ph.D. in Computer Engineering from NTUA in 2022. From 2023 to 2025, she was a postdoctoral researcher in the Department of Computer Science at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. Before and during her doctoral studies, she held research positions at Chalmers University of Technology and the University of Wisconsin–Madison, and spent a year in industry at ZeroPoint Technologies.

Sotiris Totomis

FORTH-ICS & University of Crete

Characterizing Synonyms and TLB Invalidations for Virtually-Indexed Virtually-Tagged Caches

Abstract

Conventional virtual memory architectures require virtual-to-physical address translation via Translation Lookaside Buffers (TLBs) for every load or store before accessing the cache. Virtually-Indexed Virtually-Tagged (VIVT) caches enable direct access via untranslated virtual addresses. Such designs can reduce cache access latency and power consumption by bypassing TLBs and deferring address translation to a deeper level of the memory hierarchy. Two inherent virtual memory behaviors hinder the effectiveness of VIVTs: 1) Synonyms that occur when different virtual addresses map to the same physical address, and 2) TLB invalidations (TLBIs) that arise when the virtual memory subsystem synchronizes TLBs during mapping modifications. This work argues that an in-depth analysis of synonyms and TLBIs is crucial to VIVT advancements. In this regard, we adapt the gem5 simulator to generate detailed memory traces of both the user’s and the OS kernel’s contexts. Additionally, we implement SynSeeker, a tool that parses these traces and reports system-level synonym and TLBI properties. Our results make the following observations: a) 4−17% of the total memory operations are synonyms that require resolution, b) synonyms stem not only from copy-on-write routines, but also from the Linux kernel’s fundamental operation of clearing pages upon initial access, and c) TLBIs are frequent, especially in I/O workloads. This work serves as the baseline of our ongoing work on the OS-VIVT cooperation.

Bio

Sotiris Totomis is a Ph.D. student at the Institute of Computer Science of the Foundation for Research and Technology - Hellas (FORTH - ICS) and the Computer Science Department of the University of Crete, advised by Asst. Prof. Vassilis Papaefstathiou. His interests include virtual memory, cache memories, and cache coherence. His current Ph.D. work focuses on the intersection of these domains, specifically how Virtually-Indexed Virtually-Tagged (VIVT) caches can reduce address translation and memory-access costs while cooperating with the operating system (OS).

Manolis Katsaragakis

NTUA

Revisiting Dynamic Memory Management in the Era of Heterogeneous Memory Systems

Abstract

The increasing complexity of modern applications and the growing diversity of computing and memory technologies are driving the adoption of heterogeneous architectures. While these systems offer new opportunities to improve performance, energy efficiency, and resource utilization, they also introduce challenges in application deployment and system optimization. Addressing these challenges calls for cross-layer approaches that account for application behavior, data characteristics, and system-level constraints. In this talk, we present an Application-to-System-to-Technology co-design approach for multi-objective optimization in heterogeneous architectures. At the application level, we explore lightweight and scalable data structure refinement techniques based on access pattern analysis to improve data representation and memory utilization. At the system level, we investigate data placement strategies that balance performance, energy consumption, and memory lifetime across heterogeneous memory technologies. We also examine runtime-aware memory management flows that leverage system-level performance information to guide data placement decisions and reduce optimization overhead. Together, these approaches demonstrate how application behavior, data representation, and runtime-aware memory management can be jointly optimized to enhance the performance, efficiency, and sustainability of heterogeneous computing systems.

Bio

Manolis Katsaragakis received his PhD in 2025 from the School of Electrical and Computer Engineering at the National Technical University of Athens (NTUA), Greece and the Faculty of Engineering Science at KU Leuven, Belgium. His doctoral research focused on optimized dynamic memory management techniques for heterogeneous memory systems. He also holds a Diploma in Electrical and Computer Engineering from NTUA, which he received in 2018. He is currently a postdoctoral researcher at the Microprocessors and Digital Systems Laboratory (Microlab) at NTUA. He has contributed to several European research projects, including AgRibot, EXA2PRO, NEPHELE, and CMOS2.0, and completed research internships at imec, Belgium, in 2021 and 2023. His research activities focus on dynamic memory management, heterogeneous systems, Edge/Cloud resource allocation and has resulted in more than 25 publications in international journals and conferences, including IEEE CLOUD, where he received a Best Paper Award, IEEE DATE, IEEE Transactions on Computers (TC), ACM Transactions on Architecture and Code Optimization (TACO), ACM Transactions on Embedded Computing Systems (TECS) and other.

Faiz Alam

Granular Labs

Secure Integration of Third-party Design in AI SoCs

Abstract

Due to increasing energy and performance gaps between general-purpose processors and hardware accelerators, clear trends for leveraging accelerators arise in various fields or workloads, such as edge devices, cloud systems, and data centers. Moreover, system integrators desire higher flexibility to deploy custom accelerators based on their performance, power, and cost constraints, where such integration can be as early as (1) at the design time when third-party intellectual properties (IPs) are used, (2) at integration/upgrade time when third-party discrete chip accelerators are used, or (3) during runtime as in reconfigurable logic.

A malicious third-party accelerator can compromise the entire system by accessing other processes’ data, overwriting OS data structures, etc. To eliminate these security ramifications, a unit similar to a memory management unit (MMU), namely an IOMMU, is typically used to scrutinize memory accesses from I/O devices, including accelerators. Still, IOMMU incurs significant performance overhead because it resides on the critical path of each I/O memory access. We propose CryptoMMU, to delegate the translation processes to accelerators, whereas the authentication of the targeted address is elegantly performed using a cryptography-based approach. Our evaluation results show that CryptoMMU improves system throughput by an average of 2.97× and 1.13× compared to the conventional IOMMU and the state-of-the-art solutions.

Bio

Faiz Alam is co-founder of Granular Labs, an Austin-based hardware startup building high-performance memory systems for Physical AI, with a dedicated R&D site at Proto Town in Lockhart, Texas. He recently earned his Ph.D. in ECE from North Carolina State University, specializing in the secure virtualization of heterogeneous architectures, where his work was recognized as Best Paper Runner-Up at MICRO 2023. During his PhD, he collaborated closely with NVIDIA Research, working on secure MMUs and Prefetchers. He also patented an XR neural inpainting architecture at Qualcomm Research. He also spent time at AMD Research working on op-cache software management.

05 The working session

Hands-on

The tutorial is designed around the actual research loop: understand the system, change one policy or mechanism, run a controlled experiment, and inspect the result. Everything runs on your own laptop or on a prepared cloud machine.

getting-started.sh 
# Get the framework
$ git clone --recursive https://github.com/CMU-SAFARI/Virtuoso.git
$ cd Virtuoso/simulator/sniper
$ sh run_example.sh

What you take home

  • A working Virtuoso installation with the tutorial configurations.
  • A mental model of how the OS and hardware pieces fit together in the simulator.
  • One end-to-end experiment you implemented, ran, and measured yourself.
  • Direct contact with the people who maintain the framework.
